Press **Ctrl+Space** anywhere - over the Timeline, over the picture in the
Viewer - and the FX console opens around your mouse: a **ring of choices**
centred on the pointer, with a **search bar** floating above it. It is the
fastest route to the things you do constantly: apply an effect, key a
property, make a layer.

If you have used Video Copilot's FX Console in After Effects, the search half
will feel familiar. If you have used Blender's pie menus, the ring will. If
you have used neither, give it a minute - it is one of those tools that feels
odd for ten minutes and then becomes the way you work.

## The ring

The ring puts each choice in a fixed **direction** rather than a fixed spot in
a list. That is the whole trick: after a few uses your hand learns "duplicate
is up" and you stop reading the menu. Press the chord, flick towards the
choice, release - done.

- **Flick, or click.** Press and drag in a direction and release: the slice in
  that direction runs, however far you dragged. Clicking a slice directly
  works too.
- **The middle cancels.** Release with the pointer still in the centre and
  nothing happens. Opening the menu costs nothing.
- **Escape closes it**, from anywhere, whatever has focus.
- A dimmed slice is one that cannot run right now. It keeps its place so the
  direction keeps its meaning.

Some slices carry a small caret: choosing one **expands the ring in place**
into a ring of its own. The centre names where you are and clicking it steps
back out (Escape steps back too, one level at a time).

## What is in the ring

The ring follows your selection, so it offers what you were probably about to
do:

| You have | The ring offers |
| --- | --- |
| An effect picked | Enable or disable it, copy it, remove it, add another |
| A layer selected | Duplicate, add effect, pre-compose, delete - plus **New ▸** and **Keyframe ▸** |
| A composition, nothing selected | The new-layer menu: solid, text, camera, adjustment, null, import |
| Nothing open | New composition, import footage |

**New ▸** expands into the same entries as the Layer ▸ New menu, in the same
order, so the two surfaces teach the same directions.

## The Keyframe slice

With a layer selected, **Keyframe ▸** expands into one slice per transform
row: Anchor point, Position, Scale, Rotation, Opacity. Choosing one:

1. plants a keyframe at the playhead, holding the value the property already
   has - nothing on screen moves;
2. brings the Timeline to the front with that row twirled open, so the key
   you just made is sitting there under the playhead.

It is the flick-sized version of opening the layer, finding the row, and
pressing its diamond. A row already keyed at this frame is not keyed twice -
choosing it just shows the row. A row driven by an expression is dimmed,
because writing keys over an expression would erase it. See
[Keyframes](/use/keyframes/) for what to do with the key once it exists.

## The search bar

The bar starts empty and shows nothing - the ring is the offer. Start typing
and a dropdown appears under the bar with everything that matches:

- **Effects first, compositions after a divider.** A composition never
  outranks an effect, however well it matches - the reason you opened this is
  nearly always an effect.
- Matching is loose: type `gau` and Gaussian blur is the top row. The letters
  only have to appear in order, so `db` finds Directional blur.
- **Enter applies the top match to every selected layer**, exactly as the
  Effect menu would. Choosing a composition brings it to the front instead.
- The arrow keys move the highlight; Escape clears the search first and
  closes second, so a mistyped query never costs you the whole console.

## The snapshot button

The camera button beside the search field saves the frame you are looking at
as a PNG - one press, no export dialogue. Snapshots land in a `Snapshots`
folder beside your saved project (or in your Pictures folder if the project
has never been saved). Use it to compare two versions of a look: snapshot,
change something, snapshot again.

## Not the command palette

**Ctrl+Shift+P** still opens the command palette, and the two answer different
questions: the palette is *every command by name*; the console is effects,
fast, plus the thing you were about to do. Both build their lists from the
same source as the menus, so they can never drift out of step with what the
menus mean.
